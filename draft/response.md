# Dear Editor

We thank both reviewers for their careful and constructive reading of our manuscript. Their comments have substantially improved the clarity and contextual framing of the work. We also thank Dr. Sujoy Ghosh for his editorial handling, and have added acknowledgement of this in the revised manuscript. Below we respond to each point in turn.

---

**Editor Comment 1** *"The manuscript would benefit from a more consistent linkage between the numerical results and their geophysical implications, particularly in the context of slab dynamics and seismic observables associated with the 410 discontinuity."*

We interpreted this as a call to anchor our kinetic regimes more explicitly within the broader literature. In response, we added revised the opening of Section 4 (Discussion) to frame our study in the context of previous work, and to distinguish our hypothesis from those already established. We also added a new paragraph at the start of Section 4.2 (Implications for Subduction Dynamics) that maps our three kinetic regimes onto existing equilibrium and compositional interpretations of 410 structure. We draw particular attention to the fact that the quasi-equilibrium regime approaches Clapeyron-slope predictions, while the intermediate and ultra-sluggish regimes depart from equilibrium in ways that are qualitatively distinguishable from water-content effects. Specifically, kinetic inhibition deepens the 410 whereas hydration shallows it, providing a concrete observational test for discriminating between competing interpretations. We also note the independent constraints on slab thermal structure, hydration state, and mineralogy that would be needed to apply this distinction to specific natural systems.

---

**Editor Comment 2** *"The relative roles of temperature versus reaction kinetics should be expressed more carefully."*

We agree and this comment also underlies Reviewer 1's Comment 2. We made revisions at one location in Section 3.2 (Structure of the 410: Displacement and Width), and at two locations in the opening of Section 4 (Discussion).

First, we revised our explanation of the driving force behind the narrow discontinuities observed in slab simulations within the ultra-sluggish regime. The original text framed rapid transformation at overstepped conditions as a consequence of a buildup of disequilibrium ($\Delta G$). In fact it is primarily a consequence of a sharp increase in the Arrhenius term after a critical temperature is attained. The physics have been clarified with references to Equation 13.

We then rewrote the opening paragraph of Section 4 (Discussion) to reframe the Arrhenius temperature-dependence of the reaction rate as the governing expression, and to explain how it produces the fundamental slab-plume asymmetry in our results. Finally, we added two new paragraphs immediately following that opening. The first draws an explicit distinction between equilibrium-based models and our kinetic framework. The second paragraph states directly that 'kinetic effects' and 'thermal effects' cannot be decoupled within our model, since what appears as disequilibrium is the direct consequence of cold temperatures exponentially suppressing reaction rates. We further explain that our single-component composition was chosen deliberately to isolate this kinetic signal from the compositional, water, and grain-size effects explored elsewhere in the literature.

**Reviewer 1, Comment 2** *"The first sentence of the discussion suggests 'first-order' control by kinetics, but temperature remains the primary control."*

This comment reflects the same conceptual ambiguity addressed under Editor Comment 2, and the revisions to Section 3.2 (Structure of the 410: Displacement and Width) and Section 4 (Discussion) opening are intended to resolve this ambiguity. The phrase 'first-order kinetic control' has been removed and replaced with language that describes what the model actually shows: that in cold environments, the Arrhenius law exponentially amplifies the sensitivity of reaction progress to temperature.

**Reviewer 1, Comment 4** *"Including a viscosity figure; comparison with the existing literature; the role of dislocation creep and slab speed."*

We addressed this through a new paragraph in Section 4.1 (Uncertainties and Model Limitations) rather than with a new figure, since the rheological model is fully defined by the equations already in the text and a viscosity profile would not convey additional information beyond what those equations already specify. The paragraph clarifies that no viscosity jump is applied at the 410, consistent with published estimates that viscosity discontinuities at the 410 are minor. We also note that grain-size-sensitive diffusion creep, which would reduce local viscosity following transformation, is intentionally absent from our rheological formulation so as not to obscure the first-order kinetic relationships we are quantifying. The discrepancy between simulated and natural slab descent rates is addressed in the same paragraph.

---

**Reviewer 2, General Comment** *"Frame findings in more phenomenological terms rather than directly extrapolating to natural cases."*

We used this framing to sharpen the overall narrative. In the revised Section 4 (Discussion) opening and in Section 4.1 (Uncertainties and Model Limitations), we now explicitly characterize our results as a phenomenological assessment of kinetic contributions to 410 structure, deliberately isolated from the compositional and rheological complexity of natural subduction zones. Following the revised opening to Section 4.1, we state this directly in a revised paragraph: by adopting a single-component monomineralic composition, we isolate the kinetic signal from Fe-Mg partitioning, water content, and lithological mixing effects that have been characterized elsewhere, and our results are not intended as direct quantitative predictions for specific natural systems.

---

**Reviewer 2, Comment 1** *"The temporal dimension of the models is not sufficiently exploited; is there periodic or cyclical behavior?"*

We acknowledge that the temporal evolution of slab kinematics and 410 morphology is a scientifically interesting direction. Our quasi-steady-state approach was a deliberate design choice. Seismic imaging provides snapshots rather than time series, and our goal was to characterize first-order kinetic and rheological controls on 410 structure systematically before exploring transient dynamics. Expanding this analysis would substantially change the scope and focus of the study, and we have not added new temporal analyses to the current manuscript.

---

**Reviewer 2, Comment 2** *"Explicitly compare hydrated versus dry cold plates; clarify how water influences each kinetic regime."*

Rather than adding explicit water content experiments, we revised the opening of Section 4.1 (Uncertainties and Model Limitations) explaining our treatment of water transparently. Water content and grain size are deliberately absorbed into the single kinetic prefactor $Z$ because mantle water concentrations remain poorly constrained and because disentangling their individual contributions is not necessary to characterize the first-order kinetic behavior the study is designed to isolate. We also revised the closing sentence to the first paragraph of Section 4.1 making explicit that our results are interpreted in terms of 'fast versus slow' kinetics rather than 'wet versus dry' conditions or 'fine versus coarse' grain sizes.

---

**Reviewer 2, Comment 3** *"Grain-size dependence of rheology; viscosity contrast between upper mantle and transition zone."*

Addressed in the revised Section 4.1 (Uncertainties and Model Limitations), as described under Reviewer 1 Comment 4 above. We note that grain-size reduction following transformation would lower local viscosity via diffusion creep and accelerate kinetics, and that incorporating these feedbacks would substantially expand the parameter space and obscure the kinetic relationships this study aims to quantify. The justification for the absence of a viscosity jump at 410 km is grounded in published viscosity profiles.

---

**Reviewer 2, Comment 4** *"No vertical depth/pressure scale in Figures 5 and 6 to facilitate comparison with seismic observations; metastable olivine in the wadsleyite or ringwoodite stability fields."*

We have addressed this in Section 4.1 (Uncertainties and Model Limitations). The new paragraph on the viscosity profile and missing phase transitions explains that the 410 transition depth evolves dynamically from the rate law rather than being pinned to a prescribed depth/pressure. Assigning an Earth-referenced depth/pressure scale would imply a reference anchor that does not exist in our model. Instead we show a spatial scale bar in each figure and outline the 410 (defined by the X field as described in the figure captions). The quantities we report, displacement and width relative to the nominal equilibrium transition depth, are precisely the quantities that seismological studies measure, making the comparison meaningful without requiring an absolute depth calibration. The paragraph also clarifies that the 520- and 660-km discontinuities are absent from the current model, so flow below the 410 should be understood accordingly.
